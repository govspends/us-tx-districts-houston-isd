# 4. State funding per student: did it actually fall?

This is the question the investigation began with. The short answer is no — but the mechanism behind why the state's share of HISD's funding *rose* sharply is itself revealing, and is not what it first appears to be.

## How the formula works

Texas funds districts through the Foundation School Program. The state sets a per-student entitlement — Tier One (the basic program) plus Tier Two (enrichment) — built from a flat per-student amount (the Basic Allotment) multiplied by weights for different student needs (special education, bilingual education, low-income students, career and technical education, and more). Local property tax revenue, at a state-set rate, is expected to cover part of that entitlement; the state fills the remainder. Where local tax revenue, at the state-set rate, would exceed the entitlement, Texas "recaptures" the excess and redistributes it to property-poor districts — this is the mechanism popularly known as "Robin Hood."

HISD's local property-tax base is wealthy enough that its local share has, in most recent years, exceeded its Tier One entitlement on its own — meaning **HISD receives $0 of Tier One state aid.** Its state money instead comes from Tier Two (a guaranteed-yield enrichment formula), the Available School Fund (a small flat per-student payment every district gets), and various add-on programs.

**The Basic Allotment — the core per-student rate — did not fall.** It rose from $6,160 to $6,215, under a 2025 state law (House Bill 2, 89th Legislature). Combined state-and-local formula funding per attending student rose modestly across the same period: $9,225 → $9,964 → $9,899.

## Why state aid jumped — and why it isn't new money

State aid to HISD nearly doubled, from $172.0 million to $313.7 million, between school years 2024-25 and 2025-26. The cause is not new funding tied to students or performance: it is the state **replacing local property-tax revenue that the Legislature itself eliminated.**

In 2023, the Texas Legislature passed a major property-tax relief package (Senate Bill 2, 88th Legislature, 2nd Called Session) that cut school districts' maximum compressed tax rate by an additional $0.107 per $100 of property value and raised the homestead exemption from $40,000 to $100,000; a further round of relief in 2025 raised the exemption again, to $140,000 (with an additional $60,000 for homeowners who are elderly or disabled). Cutting the local tax rate shrinks the amount local property taxes are expected to raise — which shrinks, and in HISD's case eliminated, the "excess" local revenue subject to recapture, and requires the state to backfill the resulting gap with new aid programs (additional compression aid, homestead-exemption hold-harmless aid, and aid for "districts no longer subject to recapture").

**TEA's own compression worksheet shows the counterfactual directly:** without the additional tax-rate compression, HISD would have owed **$156.3 million in recapture** for school year 2026-27. Instead it owes $0, and is receiving the equivalent in state aid.

**This is not driven by HISD losing students**, as might be assumed — if anything, a falling entitlement from fewer students would tend to push recapture *up*, not down, since the formula compares a (now smaller) entitlement against local tax revenue that doesn't automatically fall with enrollment. The driver is specifically the state's own tax-rate legislation, confirmed directly from TEA's worksheet rather than inferred.

## What's actually true, if funding feels tighter

If someone says HISD is getting "less per student" from the state, two things in this data are real, even though the headline claim is not:

- **Total formula dollars do shrink as attendance falls**, because the formula is attendance-based: HISD's own FY2027 budget assumes about $50 million less in state aid from a projected 4,000-student enrollment decline.
- **The Basic Allotment has lost ground to inflation.** It rose only $55 (less than 1%) between 2019 and 2025, while the cost of running schools rose considerably more over the same period.

Neither of those is what drove the headline numbers in the table above; the compression-and-backfill mechanism is the dominant story.
