# 6. NES vs. non-NES schools

As part of the Texas Education Agency's 2023 takeover of HISD, the state-appointed administration introduced the "New Education System" (NES) — a reform model with standardized, centrally built staffing and a different pay structure, applied to a growing subset of HISD campuses. It is the single clearest fault line in how HISD's money is distributed among its own schools.

## The spending gap

| Per student | NES campuses | Non-NES campuses | Gap |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| **Actual** operating spending, 2024-25, all funds (TEA data) | **$14,288** | **$9,193** | $5,095 |
| Same, General Fund only | $12,460 | $8,437 | $4,023 |
| Same campuses, 2022-23, before NES began | $10,125 | $8,421 | $1,704 |
| **Budgeted** campus allocation, FY2025 (HISD's own budget pages) | $9,453 | $6,903 | $2,550 |
| **Budgeted** campus allocation, FY2026 | $10,627 | $7,118 | $3,509 |
| HISD's own FY2026 "base funding" figure | $8,566 | $6,133 | $2,433 |

<p class="caption">Sources: TEA campus-level PEIMS actual financial data, 2022-23 through 2024-25; HISD's adopted budget "School Pages," FY2025-FY2026.</p>

The gap in actual spending — $5,095 per student in 2024-25 — is about three times what it was before the reform began. Part of this reflects school size (NES campuses tend to be smaller, and fixed staffing costs like an assistant principal cost the same regardless of enrollment, so smaller schools look more expensive per student); part of it reflects a deliberate, publicly stated funding priority for NES campuses.

## Enrollment is falling faster at NES campuses

| Campus group | Enrollment change, 2022-23 → 2024-25 |
|---|---:|
| Original 28 NES schools (2023 cohort) | −10.9% |
| NES-aligned schools | −14.1% |
| Schools that joined NES in 2024-25 | −12.6% |
| Non-NES schools | −2.6% |

<p class="caption">Source: TEA campus-level membership data.</p>

NES campuses are losing students four to five times faster than the rest of the district. The investigation did not establish a causal link between the funding model and the enrollment decline; both could share a common cause (NES campuses were disproportionately low-performing campuses to begin with, which may itself drive enrollment loss independent of the reform).

## District-wide NES budget totals (HISD's own figures)

| School year | NES campus budget | Number of NES campuses |
|---|---:|---:|
| FY2024 | $513.4M | 85 |
| FY2025 | $732.2M | 130 |
| FY2026 | $545.3M | — |

**There is no separate state funding stream for NES.** The Texas Education Agency has not created a distinct Foundation School Program allotment for intervention districts implementing a reform model; HISD's superintendent has publicly stated that intervention districts "should get a little extra money, but we haven't." The NES funding differential is paid for entirely out of HISD's own budget — through internal reallocation away from non-NES campuses and central-office functions, and by drawing down the district's reserves (see [Section 5](05-budget-deficit-and-reserves.md)).

**FY2027 brings a formula change.** Starting FY2027, HISD is moving to a new, uniform per-student funding formula for all campuses — a $1,400 base amount plus state-style weights for student need — with NES campuses receiving an additional $500 per student on top.

**Caveat on this data.** TEA's campus-level actual-spending data covers only about 80% of HISD's total operating spending; central/districtwide costs are not attributed down to individual campuses, so the true all-in gap (including central support specifically directed at NES campuses) is not fully captured here.
