<p class="kicker">Houston Independent School District, Texas</p>

# Funding and Spending, FY2023-FY2027

<p class="lead">Where HISD's money comes from, where it goes, and whether the claim that the state cut its per-student funding holds up against the primary data.</p>

**Prepared:** October 6-7, 2026. **Basis:** TEA's Summary of Finances reports for HISD (school years 2024-25 through 2026-27), HISD's audited Annual Comprehensive Financial Reports (FY2022-FY2025), HISD's adopted budgets (FY2025-FY2027) and in-year amendments, TEA's PEIMS actual-spending and campus-level data, TAPR staffing data, School FIRST ratings, Federal Audit Clearinghouse single-audit records, and EMMA bond official statements. Every figure is traceable to a document listed in [References](references.md); source files are saved alongside this report. **This first edition has not yet been independently reviewed** — see [the audit summary](audit-summary.md) for known open items.

**Fiscal years:** HISD's fiscal year runs July 1 to June 30; "FY2025" is the year ending June 30, 2025 (school year 2024-25). Texas funds districts through "school years" (SY) on the same July-June calendar. This is an independent analysis and is not affiliated with Houston ISD or the Texas Education Agency.

## 1. Summary of findings

**Did the state cut HISD's per-student funding?** No — this is the question that started the investigation, and the primary data does not support it. The Basic Allotment (the core state per-student rate) rose from $6,160 to $6,215. Combined state-and-local formula funding per attending student rose from $9,225 (school year 2024-25) to $9,964 (2025-26) to a projected $9,899 (2026-27). State aid itself rose sharply, from $172.0 million to $313.7 million, but mostly because the state replaced local property tax revenue that the Legislature cut in 2023 — not because of new money tied to enrollment or performance. [Section 4](04-state-funding-per-student.md) has the full decomposition.

**The real financial pressure is on the spending side, not the state.** HISD's General Fund has spent more than it took in every year since the state's 2023 takeover, and the deficits have grown each year:

| | FY2023 | FY2024 | FY2025 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| General Fund deficit | −$20.2M | −$166.6M | **−$310.6M** |

In FY2025 the district spent $2.251 billion against $1.940 billion of revenue, and finished **$74.7 million over its own final budget**.

**Reserves fell $379 million in two years.** The General Fund balance went from $1.127 billion (June 2023) to $748.1 million (June 2025). The unassigned portion, $464.8 million, is 2.48 months of spending — below the board's own 3-month reserve policy. HISD twice committed publicly to keeping at least $800 million and did not. [Section 5](05-budget-deficit-and-reserves.md).

**Federal COVID relief ran out, and the General Fund absorbed the cost.** Federal revenue fell from $816 million (FY2023) to $401 million (FY2025). Over the same two years, General Fund instruction spending rose 22% and school leadership spending rose 44%, while enrollment fell 7%.

**HISD spends more per student than the state average, concentrated in campus leadership.** 2024-25 operating spending was $14,147 per student — 9% above the state average, on par with Dallas and Austin, and 21-26% above the fast-growing Houston-area suburban districts. School-leadership spending is 1.8 times the state rate, and HISD has the fewest teachers per student of the eight large districts examined. [Section 3](03-where-the-money-goes.md).

**Schools in the state-mandated New Education System (NES) reform get far more money per student, and are losing students the fastest.** The 130 NES campuses spent $14,288 per student in 2024-25, versus $9,193 at other campuses — a gap that was only $1,704 before the reform began. NES campuses lost 11-14% of their enrollment from 2022-23 to 2024-25, against 2.6% elsewhere. There is no separate state funding for NES; it runs on internal reallocation and the district's reserves. [Section 6](06-nes-vs-non-nes.md).

**Debt outstanding is falling, but new borrowing continues without a voter election.** Bonds and notes fell from $2.12 billion to $1.51 billion between FY2023 and FY2025. After voters rejected a $4.4 billion bond proposal in November 2024, HISD issued $114.4 million of maintenance-tax notes (which do not require a vote) and the board authorized up to $182.5 million more in lease-revenue bonds. A 2026 debt refinancing pushes FY2027 debt payments to $438.5 million — more than the dedicated debt-service tax raises. [Section 7](07-federal-funds-debt.md).

**Several figures disagree across HISD's own and TEA's documents** and remain unreconciled — most notably HISD's FY2025 recapture payment, reported as five different amounts in five different sources. [Section 8](08-figures-that-disagree.md) lists every one found.
